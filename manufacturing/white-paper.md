# Manufacturing Policy for India

> Status: working white-paper draft. This is the control document for the manufacturing paper. The short essay remains in [Industrial Base Math](./industrial-base-math.md); this file is where the research-grade argument is being built.

## Abstract

India should treat manufacturing as the main exception to a normally permission-light economic state. The argument is not nostalgia for factories or a return to the License Raj. It is that manufacturing creates learning externalities, supplier capabilities, export discipline, strategic options, and formal employment ladders that private firms cannot fully capture. A policy regime built around factory-time metrics, qualifying zero-tax treatment for new manufacturing profits, export discipline, and low-discretion state competition may fit India's state capacity better than discretionary winner-picking.

This paper develops that claim through five lenses: learning externalities, misallocation and the missing middle, premature deindustrialization, comparative industrialization cases, and Indian sectoral bottlenecks.

## Research Questions

The paper should answer four questions precisely enough that critics can attack the assumptions rather than the form:

1. When does manufacturing deserve a policy wedge on welfare-theoretic grounds?
2. Which Indian manufacturing constraints are binding: tax, logistics, finance, labor thresholds, power, land, customs, supplier depth, or state capability?
3. Why is a broad, rule-based zero-tax regime superior to PLI-style fiscal transfers for most manufacturing formation?
4. What institutional design turns manufacturing rents into capability accumulation rather than capture?

The answer is not "more state." The answer is a narrow state exception, built for an Indian administrative reality where audit, registration, and public measurement are more plausible than discretionary winner-picking.

## 1. Executive Doctrine

The doctrine to be tested:

- small state for daily life;
- strong state for industrial capacity;
- free markets on national infrastructure;
- zero tax for qualifying new manufacturing capacity;
- factory-time as the operational measure of ease of doing business.

The burden of proof is high. A serious paper must show why manufacturing is special, why India is constrained, why broad low-discretion instruments are preferable to discretionary industrial policy, and how capture will be contained.

## 2. Contribution

The paper's intended contribution is a synthesis, not a claim of theoretical novelty.

It should connect:

- a learning-externality foundation from Greenwald-Stiglitz;
- a sector-choice apparatus from Hausmann-Hidalgo and Liu;
- an Indian constraint diagnosis from Hsieh-Klenow, Besley-Burgess, Kelkar-Shah, and Pritchett;
- a comparative discipline mechanism from Korea, Taiwan, Vietnam, Bangladesh, and China;
- a low-discretion policy design: zero tax for qualifying new manufacturing profits, audited domestic value addition, factory-time dashboards, export discipline, and automatic sunset.

The distinctive move is to argue that a right-wing, market-first Indian state can still make manufacturing the exception because the exception is rule-bound, measurable, and capacity-building. The policy is not a license to plan the economy. It is a way to stop taxing the formation of industrial capability while making states compete on the time it takes to build and run factories.

## 4. Formal Model

The model chapter should replace napkin arithmetic with explicit assumptions. The static fiscal intuition in the note is useful, but it is only a corner case. The full model needs three layers.

First, derive the policy wedge from learning externalities:

```text
private return = current-period producer surplus
social return = private return + learning spillovers + supplier capability + strategic option value
```

If the spillover term is positive and firms cannot appropriate it, the optimal wedge is positive. Under severe state-capacity constraints, `tau_c = 0` for qualifying profits can be treated as an administratively simple approximation to a more precise learning subsidy.

Second, build a two-stock capability model:

```text
K_t = physical capital stock
H_t = production capability stock
H_{t+1} = H_t + phi(Q_t, supplier_density_t, export_discipline_t) - delta_H H_t
```

Third, simulate four regimes: baseline, PLI-only, broad zero tax, and broad zero tax plus narrow strategic-capacity premia. Outputs should include cumulative domestic value added, fiscal cost, formal payroll, exports, and capability stock after 10 and 20 years.

## 5. India in the Product Space

This section should use the economic-complexity frame to distinguish sectors within reach from aspirational sectors. The paper should compare India with Vietnam, China, and Bangladesh using ECI trajectory, product-space density, and revealed comparative advantage transitions.

The policy consequence is sector discipline. Electronics assembly, pharma APIs, apparel, auto components, specialty steel, batteries, semiconductor ATMP, and wafer fabs are not the same policy problem. A right industrial policy for India should separate adjacent capability bets from frontier strategic bets.

## 6. Misallocation and the Missing Middle

Anchor: Hsieh and Klenow's estimate that reducing Indian manufacturing misallocation to U.S.-like levels could raise Indian manufacturing TFP by roughly 40-60%.

This section should connect that result to:

- size-dependent distortions;
- informality and threshold avoidance;
- finance constraints;
- state-level regulatory variation.

The 100,000-manufacturer target must not become a subsidy for smallness. It should mean many more firms capable of crossing scale thresholds, formalizing payroll, joining supplier networks, and exporting.

## 7. Premature Deindustrialization

Anchor: Rodrik's finding that developing countries are reaching peak industrialization earlier and at lower income levels than early industrializers. India's demographic window should be reframed as a deindustrialization window.

This section should compare manufacturing employment and value-added shares across India, Korea, Taiwan, Malaysia, China, Vietnam, and Bangladesh at comparable income levels. The core urgency claim is that India cannot wait for "basics first" to finish before manufacturing formation begins. The demographic window and manufacturing window are closing together.

## 8. Comparative Case Studies

Required cases:

- Korea HCI drive, 1973-1979;

Each case should follow:

The case-study chapter should identify what India can copy, what India cannot copy, and what must be adapted to a federal democratic state with weaker centralized discipline.

## 10. Political Economy and State Capability

This is the chapter that must prevent the proposal from becoming romantic industrial policy. It should use Khan, Kelkar-Shah, Pritchett, Andrews, Woolcock, and state-level Indian evidence.

Core question:

What turns manufacturing rents into capability accumulation rather than rent dissipation?

The answer cannot be "trust the state." The answer has to be institutional design: low-discretion eligibility, automatic sunset, audited domestic value addition, export discipline where relevant, public dashboards, independent audit, and revocation for non-performance.

## 11. Federal Architecture

Manufacturing is substantially state-mediated. This chapter will compare Tamil Nadu, Gujarat, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Telangana, Uttar Pradesh, and other relevant states. The proposal should specify what belongs to the Union, what belongs to states, and what needs inter-state coordination.

The likely architecture is a Manufacturing Council with GST-Council-style coordination but narrower scope: factory-time metrics, land-bank transparency, industrial power tariffs, labor-code rollout, logistics corridors, and common audit standards. The goal is race-to-the-top competition on time, reliability, and throughput rather than race-to-the-bottom fiscal concessions.

## 12. Strategic Capacity as Real Option

Broad zero-tax manufacturing policy should be separated from narrow strategic-capacity premia for sectors where geopolitical option value is high: APIs, mature-node semiconductors, rare earths, drones, telecom/RAN, grid components, and batteries.

The real-options chapter should value domestic capacity under geopolitical volatility. Some sectors deserve a premium not because they maximize near-term employment, but because the option value of having domestic production rises sharply under supply shock, sanctions risk, war, pandemic, or export-control regimes.

## 13. Objections

The paper must steelman:

- broad liberalization beats industrial policy;
- rent-seeking dominates learning;
- the Indian state lacks execution bandwidth;
- basics-first should precede industrial ambition.

The Kelkar-Shah/Pritchett objection is the hardest and should get the longest treatment. If India cannot run discretionary industrial policy well, the paper must show why this proposal relies on lower-order state capabilities: registration, audit, tax administration, customs data, payroll records, and public dashboards.

## 14. Policy Package

Likely components:

- qualifying zero tax for new manufacturing profits;
- factory-time dashboard;
- export discipline for deeper benefits;
- domestic value-addition thresholds;
- sunset and audit architecture;
- strategic-capacity premia;
- Manufacturing Council for federal coordination.

## 15. Data and Replication Standard

The paper should keep every numerical claim traceable. Minimum data work:

- DGCI&S bilateral trade by HS code;
- ASI industry/plant data where available;
- PLI approvals, disbursements, sales, exports, and employment by scheme;
- tariff schedules and input-output tables for effective protection;
- EPFO/ESI payroll data for wage-side fiscal capture;
- port dwell time, customs clearance, power connection, and land allotment data;
- state industrial-policy incentives and realized factory-time metrics.

Every table should identify source, year, unit, transformation, and uncertainty. Rounded estimates are better than fake precision.
