# Small State for Daily Life. Strong State for Manufacturing.

The in/acc state should be libertarian by default, conservative in social foundations, and industrial in manufacturing.

For most of life, the state should step back. Protect property. Enforce contracts. Reduce licenses. Cut compliance. Lower taxes where possible. Let families, firms, communities, markets, and civil society organize themselves. The default posture should be market-first and permission-light.

But manufacturing is different.

Manufacturing is not just another sector. It is national capacity. A software company can begin with laptops, talent, cloud credits, and distribution. A factory cannot be conjured by deregulation alone. It needs land, power, water, roads, rail, ports, warehouses, customs, standards, credit, tooling, testing, worker housing, supplier clusters, and predictable dispute resolution.

These are not things a single entrepreneur can solve from first principles. They are coordination problems at civilizational scale.

So the right-wing position should not be "the state must do nothing." It should be: the state must do only what only the state can do.

For most sectors: deregulate.

For manufacturing: coordinate.

For most entrepreneurs: get out of the way.

For factories: build the conditions.

For markets: allow competition.

For industrial capacity: seed, connect, and defend it.

The state should not run factories like the License Raj. It should not pick every winner, set every price, or bury producers under permissions. But it must act as the industrial platform builder: serviced land, reliable power, logistics corridors, ports, worker housing, skilling pipelines, shared testing labs, procurement support, export facilitation, and fast dispute resolution.

This is not socialism. It is civilizational statecraft.

## Ease of Doing Business Must Mean Factory-Time

For manufacturing, "ease of doing business" should not mean a slogan or a ranking. It should mean a measurable reduction in the time, cost, uncertainty, and friction required to turn capital into production.

A state that claims to support manufacturing should measure itself by hard operational metrics:

| Metric | Target |
|--------|--------|
| Time to acquire or lease industrial land | Under 30 days |
| Time to receive factory construction approvals | Under 45 days |
| Time to get an industrial power connection | Under 15 days |
| Industrial power uptime | 99.9%+ |
| Time for GST, labor, pollution, and local registrations | Under 7 days |
| Physical office visits required for routine compliance | 0 |
| Separate compliance filings per year for MSME manufacturers | Under 12 |
| Customs clearance for imported inputs | 24-48 hours |
| Government refunds, dues, and approved subsidies | Under 30 days |
| Commercial contract enforcement | Under 180 days |
| Payment-delay resolution from large buyers or government | Under 45 days |
| Inspection or rejection dispute resolution | Under 15 days |
| Company incorporation to first production run | 90-120 days |

The master metric is simple:

**Days from intent to first production.**

A factory does not care whether a portal exists. A factory cares whether it can start producing. If an entrepreneur has capital, designs, workers, machines, and customers, the state's job is to make sure nothing artificial blocks the path from land to machine installation to first shipment.

The manufacturing test is practical:

Can I get land without political brokerage?

Can I get power without bribery or delay?

Can I import inputs without customs uncertainty?

Can I hire workers without legal traps?

Can I move goods to ports and cities cheaply?

Can I comply digitally without harassment?

Can I receive payments on time?

Can I enforce contracts quickly?

Can I expand capacity without starting permissions from zero?

Can I export without drowning in paperwork?

If the answer is no, the state is not pro-manufacturing, no matter how many investor summits it hosts.

## Zero Tax for Manufacturing Is Import-Substitution Arithmetic

India is already paying a manufacturing subsidy. It is just paying it to foreign industrial bases.

India's goods trade deficit with China has hovered around the $100 billion scale, with recent estimates ranging from roughly $99 billion in FY 2024-25 to over $110 billion by calendar-year measures. This is not only a trade number. It is a measure of factories not built in India, workers not trained in India, suppliers not created in India, and industrial learning not accumulated in India.

Now compare three choices.

| Policy | What India Pays | Who Gets It | What India Gets |
|--------|-----------------|-------------|-----------------|
| Current model | Around $100B+ annual trade deficit | Foreign industrial base | Goods, but jobs and supply chains stay outside India |
| PLI | Budgeted subsidy | Selected firms | Some production, but scheme-driven and bureaucratic |
| Zero tax for manufacturing | Tax foregone only when factories earn | All qualifying manufacturers | Domestic capacity, jobs, exports, supplier clusters |

The math is simple.

Suppose India replaces $20 billion of imports with domestic manufacturing.

Assume:

```text
Domestic production: $20B
Manufacturer profit margin: 10%
Tax rate otherwise: 25%
Corporate tax foregone:
$20B x 10% x 25% = $500M
```

The state gives up $500 million of tax only after $20 billion of domestic production exists.

That is a 40:1 ratio.

Even if domestic value addition is only 40%:

```text
Domestic production: $20B
Domestic value added: 40%
Indian GDP value created: $8B
Tax foregone: $500M
```

That is still $8 billion of domestic value creation for $500 million of forgone corporate tax, or 16:1.

PLI is better than doing nothing, but zero tax is cleaner.

PLI says: apply, qualify, comply, wait, prove incremental production, receive incentive.

Zero tax says: manufacture, sell, earn, keep the profit, reinvest.

PLI rewards selected output. Zero tax rewards every profitable manufacturer.

PLI needs bureaucratic discretion. Zero tax can be rule-based.

PLI pays firms from the budget. Zero tax only gives up tax after value has already been created.

So the policy should be direct:

**Tax consumption, land speculation, monopoly rents, and luxury extraction if needed. Do not tax new manufacturing capacity.**

For 10-15 years, make manufacturing profits tax-free if firms meet clear conditions:

- physical production in India
- audited payroll and formal employment
- minimum domestic value addition thresholds
- digital compliance
- labor and environmental standards
- reinvestment into capacity, tooling, R&D, exports, or worker training

The state should say:

**If you manufacture in India, employ Indians, build supply chains, and reduce strategic imports, your profit is yours. Scale first. Tax later.**

## The Target: 100,000 Manufacturers

The goal should be explicit: seed 10,000 manufacturers, then grow them into 100,000.

If each manufacturer supports 100 direct jobs, that is 10 million productive, tax-paying jobs, and many more indirect jobs across suppliers, logistics, repair, packaging, distribution, design, maintenance, and services.

Every physical product is not merely an object. It is a node in an industrial culture.

The state should help small manufacturers with serviced land, reliable power, shared testing facilities, common tooling centers, working-capital access, export support, fast clearances, logistics corridors, digital compliance, supplier discovery, and procurement guarantees.

Build the roads, but also make sure there are goods to move on them. Expressways without factories become monuments. Expressways connected to manufacturers become industrial arteries.

Without manufacturing, India has consumers but not enough producers. It has roads but not enough goods. It has talent but not enough industrial absorption. It has ambition but not enough strategic depth.

A civilization that does not manufacture is dependent on those that do.
